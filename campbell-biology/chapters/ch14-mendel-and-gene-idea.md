## Key Concepts
### Concept 14.1: Mendel used the scientific approach to
- Gregor Mendel formulated a theory of inheritance based on experiments with garden peas, proposing that parents pass on to their offspring discrete genes that retain their identity through generations. This theory includes two “laws.”
- The law of segregation states that genes have alternative forms, or alleles. In a diploid organism, the two alleles of a gene segregate (separate) during meiosis and gamete formation; each sperm or egg carries only one allele of each pair. This law explains the 3:1 ratio of F2 phenotypes observed when monohybrids cross-or self-pollinate. Each organism inherits one allele for each gene from each parent. In heterozygotes, the two alleles are different: Expression of the dominant allele masks the phenotypic effect of the recessive allele. Homozygotes have identical alleles of a given gene and are therefore true-breeding.
- The law of independent assortment states that the pair of alleles for a given gene segregates into gametes independently of the pair of alleles for any other gene. In a cross between dihybrids (individuals heterozygous for two genes), the offspring have four phenotypes in a 9:3:3:1 ratio.

### Concept 14.2: Probability
- The multiplication rule states that the probability of two or more events occurring together (a statement using and) is equal to the product of the individual probabilities of the independent single events. The addition rule states that the probability of an event that can occur in two or more independent, mutually exclusive ways (using or) is the sum of the individual probabilities.
- The rules of probability can be used to solve complex genetics problems. A dihybrid or other multicharacter cross is equivalent to two or more independent monohybrid crosses occurring simultaneously. In calculating the chances of the various offspring genotypes from such crosses, each character is first considered separately and then the individual probabilities are multiplied. ## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming Dominant Alleles Are Inherently More Common**: Allele frequency in a population depends on natural selection and evolutionary mechanisms, not on whether an allele is dominant or recessive (e.g. polydactyly is dominant but rare).
- **Confusing Sister Chromatids with Homologous Chromosomes**: Sister chromatids are identical duplicate copies of a single chromosome; homologous chromosomes are maternal/paternal pairs carrying matching genes with potentially different alleles.
- **Assuming 1 Gene = 1 Protein**: Alternative pre-mRNA splicing and post-translational proteolytic cleavage allow a single eukaryotic gene locus to encode dozens of distinct protein isoforms.

## Worked Example
### Worked Example: Multi-Locus Dihybrid Cross & Probability Calculations
**Problem**: In garden peas, purple flower color ($P$) is dominant to white ($p$), and round seeds ($R$) are dominant to wrinkled ($r$). In a cross between two dihybrids ($PpRr \times PpRr$), what is the probability of obtaining an offspring that has white flowers and round seeds?
**Solution via Probability Laws**:
1. Deconstruct dihybrid cross into two independent monohybrid crosses:
   - Flower color: $Pp \times Pp \implies 1/4\, PP, 1/2\, Pp, 1/4\, pp$.
     Probability of white flowers (phenotype $pp$) $= \frac{1}{4}$.
   - Seed shape: $Rr \times Rr \implies 1/4\, RR, 1/2\, Rr, 1/4\, rr$.
     Probability of round seeds (phenotype $R\_$) $= \frac{3}{4}$.
2. Apply the **Multiplication Rule** for independent assortment:
   $$P(\text{white and round}) = P(pp) \times P(R\_) = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{16}$$
**Alternative Check**: From the classical 9:3:3:1 dihybrid ratio:
- 9 Purple-Round : 3 Purple-Wrinkled : 3 White-Round : 1 White-Wrinkled $\implies 3/16$ White-Round.
