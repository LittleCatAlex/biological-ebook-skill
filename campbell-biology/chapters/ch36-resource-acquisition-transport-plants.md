## Frameworks Introduced
- **Transpiration-Cohesion-Tension Xylem Transport**
  - **When to use**: Explaining long-distance negative-pressure water ascent in vascular plants
  - **How**: Evaporative water loss at stomata creates negative hydrostatic pressure (tension) in leaf mesophyll; hydrogen bonding cohesion transmits this pull downward through continuous xylem vessels.
- **Pressure-Flow Translocation in Phloem**
  - **When to use**: Explaining positive-pressure bulk flow of photoassimilates from source to sink
  - **How**: Active loading of sucrose at source tissues lowers water potential, drawing water into sieve tubes; high turgor pressure pushes sap toward sinks where sucrose is unloaded.

## Key Concepts
### Concept 36.1: Adaptations for acquiring resources were key
- Core principle: Adaptations for acquiring resources were key. Regulates physiological, genetic, or environmental processes essential to resource acquisition and transport in vascular plants.

### Concept 36.2: Different mechanisms transport substances
- The selective permeability of the plasma membrane controls the movement of substances into and out of cells. Both active and passive transport mechanisms occur in plants.
- Plant tissues have two major compartments: the apoplast (everything outside the cells’ plasma membranes) and the symplast (the cytosol and connecting plasmodesmata).
- Direction of water movement depends on the water potential, a quantity that incorporates solute concentration and physical pressure. The osmotic uptake of water by plant cells and the resulting internal pressure that builds up make plant cells turgid.
- Long-distance transport occurs through bulk flow, the movement of liquid in response to a pressure gradient. Bulk flow occurs within the tracheids and vessel elements of the xylem and within the sieve-tube elements of the phloem.

### Concept 36.3: Transpiration drives the transport of water and
- Water and minerals from the soil enter the plant through the epidermis of roots, cross the root cortex, and then pass into the vascular cylinder by way of the selectively permeable cells of the endodermis. From the vascular cylinder, the xylem sap is transported long distances by bulk flow to the veins that branch throughout each leaf.
- The cohesion-tension hypothesis proposes that the movement of xylem sap is driven by a water potential difference created at the leaf end of the xylem by the evaporation of water from leaf cells. Evaporation lowers the water potential at the airwater interface, thereby generating the negative pressure that pulls water through the xylem.

### Concept 36.4: The rate of transpiration is regulated by
- Transpiration is the loss of water vapor from plants. Wilting occurs when the water lost by transpiration is not replaced by absorption from roots. Plants respond to water deficits by closing their stomata. Under prolonged drought conditions, plants can become irreversibly injured.
- Stomata are the major pathway for water loss from plants. A stoma opens when guard cells bordering the stomatal pore take up K+. The opening and closing of stomata are controlled by light, CO2, the drought hormone abscisic acid, and a circadian rhythm.
- Xerophytes are plants that are adapted to arid environments. Reduced leaves and CAM photosynthesis are examples of adaptations to arid environments.

### Concept 36.5: Sugars are transported from sources to sinks
- Mature leaves are the main sugar sources, although storage organs can be seasonal sources. Growing organs such as roots, stems, and fruits are the main sugar sinks. The direction of phloem transport is always from sugar source to sugar sink.
- Phloem loading depends on the active transport of sucrose. Sucrose is cotransported with H+, which diffuses down a gradient generated by proton pumps. Loading of sugar at the source and unloading at the sink maintain a pressure difference that keeps phloem sap flowing through a sieve tube.

- Plasmodesmata can change in permeability and number. When dilated, they provide a passageway for the symplastic transport of proteins, RNAs, and other macromolecules over long distances. The phloem also conducts nerve-like electrical signals that help integrate whole-plant function.

## Mental Models
- Think of water potential $\Psi = \Psi_s + \Psi_p$ as a thermodynamic potential energy gradient; water ALWAYS flows spontaneously from higher $\Psi$ to lower $\Psi$.
- Use the primary meristem vs secondary meristem model: apical meristems lengthen shoots and roots; lateral meristems (vascular and cork cambium) thicken woody axes.
- Think of plant defense as a two-tier immune system: PAMP-triggered immunity (surface recognition) and Effector-triggered immunity (intracellular hypersensitive response & systemic acquired resistance).

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing Plants Push Water from Roots (Capillarity Fallacy)**: Root pressure rarely exceeds 0.2 MPa and cannot push water to treetop heights; transpiration pull generates negative pressures up to -2.0 MPa to lift water 100 meters.
- **Confusing Photoperiod Day Length with Night Length**: Flowering in short-day and long-day plants is strictly controlled by the duration of the UNINTERRUPTED dark period (critical night length).
- **Assuming All Plant Cells Have Chloroplasts**: Root cells, internal stem parenchyma, and floral petals lack functional chloroplasts and rely entirely on sucrose translocated from photosynthetic leaves.

## Worked Example
### Worked Example: Calculating Water Potential in Plant Cells
**Problem**: A plant flaccid cell with solute potential $\Psi_s = -0.7\text{ MPa}$ and pressure potential $\Psi_p = 0\text{ MPa}$ is placed in an open beaker of sucrose solution with $\Psi = -0.3\text{ MPa}$. Determine the direction of water movement and the equilibrium cell $\Psi_p$.
**Step-by-Step Solution**:
1. Calculate initial water potential of the cell:
   $$\Psi_{\text{cell}} = \Psi_s + \Psi_p = -0.7\text{ MPa} + 0\text{ MPa} = -0.7\text{ MPa}$$
2. Water potential of beaker solution:
   $$\Psi_{\text{solution}} = -0.3\text{ MPa}$$
3. Determine movement direction:
   $$\Psi_{\text{solution}} (-0.3\text{ MPa}) > \Psi_{\text{cell}} (-0.7\text{ MPa})$$
   Water flows down its potential gradient: **from solution into the cell**.
4. At dynamic equilibrium, $\Psi_{\text{cell}} = \Psi_{\text{solution}} = -0.3\text{ MPa}$.
   Assuming cell volume changes are negligible, $\Psi_s$ remains $-0.7\text{ MPa}$:
   $$\Psi_{\text{cell}} = \Psi_s + \Psi_p \implies -0.3\text{ MPa} = -0.7\text{ MPa} + \Psi_p \implies \Psi_p = +0.4\text{ MPa}$$
**Conclusion**: Water influx generates a positive hydrostatic turgor pressure of $+0.4\text{ MPa}$, rendering the cell fully turgid and mechanically supportive.
